Software Development Methodologies


When pursuing software development as a career, the approach used to bring a product to market can be just as important as the methods used to implement that product. 

Software development can be challenging to manage due to 

    changing business need and stakeholder requirements
    technology upgrades
    Legal and regulatory updates
    cross-functional collaboration
    competing software solution release

Certainly, the software industry is very dynamic and requires constant updating. The common thinking is that it’s better to have an approach in mind than to have none at all. A development model or methodology is just a fancy word for the approach an organization uses to structure software creation. 





### Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC)

Stakeholders determine project goals and a methodology is selected to achieve those goals. 

Such stakeholders include:
    customers
    internal and external (technical or business) experts
    development managers

A mature methodology, known as a Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC), is a structured process intended to guide a project timeline and manage risk.

The goal of a given methodology is to ensure software development is
    cost-effective
    time-effective
    meets stakeholder expectations

The SDLC provides a systematic management framework which outlines a series of steps that divide the software development process into tasks to assign, complete, and measure with specific deliverables at every stage of the software development process.

Through clear documentation, stakeholders can collectively understand the direction and progress of a project.

Some benefits of SDLC:

    Increased visibility of the development process for all stakeholders involved
    Efficient estimation, planning, and scheduling
    Improved risk management and cost estimation
    Systematic software delivery and better customer satisfaction



Key factors driving SDLC model evolution

    Accelerate software delivery, and reduce time to market
    Continuously incorporate customer feedback
    Reduce project risk and cost
    Increase productivity, collaboration, and business alignment
    Enhance software quality and security
    Frequent updates to complex infrastructure without disruptions (No-downtime)
    Increase automation
    Personnel/Developer/Skill shortage
    Rapid evolution of technologies: hybrid clouds, containers, Low code development platforms, 5G, IoT, AI, Big Data



### Emergence

Beginning in the 1960s, software engineering was recognized as a separate field of engineering. 

Margaret H. Hamilton is the person who came up with the idea of naming the discipline, software engineering, as a way of giving it legitimacy during the development of the Apollo Guidance Computer in the early 1960's.

To overcome the problems related to unstructured software development efforts, projects began to take on specific stages.

The first known presentation describing the use of such phases in software engineering was held by Herbert D. Benington at the Symposium on Advanced Programming Methods for Digital Computers 1956.

Software development methodology frameworks did not emerge until the 1960s. 
with the goal to help system developers be more productive, on target, and create higher quality code. 



### General Phases

The software development lifecycle (SDLC) is typically characterized by the specific steps or phases used to manage it. Despite the differences, some general phases remain common in most approaches.


Plan: understand the client's requirements and project objectives. 
Design: determine how the system will work, its architecture, and how it will meet the requirements.
Implement: Write system code based on the design and ensure the software meets requirements.
Deploy: Release the software to clients and support it's adoption
Maintain: Ensure that the software continues to work properly and make necessary updates over time.


There are many approaches to software project management. Not every project will require that these phases be sequentially executed. However, the phases are interdependent. Depending upon the size and complexity of the project, phases may be combined or may overlap.





### Timeline of Methodologies

SDLCs began evolving in the early days of computing, primarily during the 1950s and 1960s, as the complexity of software systems grew and the need for structured approaches became more apparent. 

Key Milestones:

    1950s-1960s: Early ad-hoc programming, beginning of structured approaches.
    1970s: Formalization of the Waterfall model and the beginning of software engineering.
    1980s-1990s: Introduction of iterative models like Spiral, and the rise of Agile methodologies.
    2000s-Present: Development of DevOps and CI/CD pipelines, modern approaches to rapid and continuous delivery.

Thus, the SDLC concept has been evolving for decades, influenced by technological advancements, the growing complexity of software, and changing business needs.



1. Pre-SDLC (Before 1950s)

    In the very early days of computing (pre-1950s), software was often written in an ad-hoc manner, with little structure or planning. Programming was a manual process, and systems were often written in machine code or early assembly languages. There was no formal understanding of a "lifecycle," and programs were written on a one-off basis to solve specific problems.

    The original approach to software development was Code-and-Fix or Big Bang Development teams wrote and released code and then fixed things that were incorrect as the customer found them. Software was delivered all at once, in a “big bang” and software developers waited to find out what they may have done wrong, both in the form of outright errors and in the form of not meeting user needs or expectations.

    This simple methodology is typically used by a single programmer working on a small project. After briefly considering the purpose of the program, the programmer codes it and runs it to see if it works. When they are done, the product is released. This methodology is useful for prototypes but cannot be used for more elaborate programs.

    This approach is a challenging way to deliver software even on the smallest, simplest scale. As the amount of code grew and became more complicated, this method was obviously too risky and expensive an approach to software development.

2. Early Structured Approaches (1950s-1960s)

    As computing technology advanced and software grew more complex, the need for more systematic approaches became clear.

    In the 1960s, the need for structured software development processes emerged, especially as software began to be used for more critical tasks (e.g., in government, military, and large businesses).

    The Waterfall model (sometimes referred to as the “linear sequential model”) was informally conceptualized in the 1960s, and is the first attempt at a formalized SDLC. This early model involved a step-by-step process where each phase was handled in sequence. This top-down model requires one step to be complete before the next begins.
    
    Variations of such models would dominated for over 2 decades. 
    However, there was no formal "Waterfall" name at that point. 

3. Dominance of the Waterfall Model (1970s)

    In 1970, Winston W. Royce published a paper describing the Waterfall model, which is often cited as one of the first formal SDLC models. Royce’s model suggested a step-by-step approach with clear phases: requirements, design, coding, testing, and maintenance.

    Though Royce himself advocated for iterative feedback within each phase, his model was commonly interpreted as a rigid, linear process in practice, forming the foundation of the traditional Waterfall SDLC.

    Waterfall Model provided a linear sequential approach to managing software projects. The waterfall model provides discipline to project management and gives a tangible output at the end of each phase. Each phase depends on deliverables from the previous one.

    The waterfall and related models while easy to manage and control did not allow for any flexibility with customer change requests, and in most cases had a long time-to-market. The strict reliance on phase order often caused delays and the revision of previous steps was not allowed. There is little room for change once a phase is considered complete, as changes can affect the software's delivery time, cost, and quality. Therefore, the model is most suitable for small software development projects, where tasks are easy to arrange and manage and requirements can be pre-defined accurately.

4. Growth of Structured Programming (1970s-1980s)

    As software development grew, so did the need for more formalized methods. This led to the rise of Structured Programming in the 1970s, which focused on improving the quality and maintainability of code. This movement emphasized dividing code into logical, manageable blocks (functions or modules).

    In parallel, Software Engineering as a discipline gained recognition, and by the late 1970s, organizations began to formalize software development into distinct phases with an emphasis on clear documentation and standards.

    V-model (1980) was an evolution of the classic waterfall methodology. The phases were executed in a sequential V-shape. A testing phase was associated with each corresponding development stage.

    The V-Model was developed as a reaction to the rigidity of the Waterfall Model. It was introduced to provide a more structured and methodical approach to both development and testing by emphasizing verification and validation throughout the process, rather than leaving it for last.

    The V-Model was mainly used for projects with well-defined and stable requirements, particularly in industries where ensuring correctness and safety was critical.

    The V-Model would maintain traction in regulated industries like aerospace, healthcare, and automotive, where compliance with standards and rigorous testing were paramount.

5. Iterative and Agile Methods (Late 1970s-1990s)

    Iterative (1975) and Incremental (1978) models attempted to establish adaptive frameworks.

    Early iterative and incremental approaches were introduced to address the limitations of Waterfall, particularly the difficulty of handling changing requirements during development.

    They involved starting with the implementation of a subset (of the software requirements) and iteratively enhancing the evolving versions until the full system was implemented. It allowed teams to start an iteration with the learnings from the previous phases. The scope of each increment was generally fixed.

    The iterative process suggests that teams begin software development with a small subset of requirements. Then, they iteratively enhance versions over time until the complete software is ready for production. The team produces a new software version at the end of each iteration.

    It’s easy to identify and manage risks, as requirements can change between iterations. However, repeated cycles could lead to scope change and underestimation of resources.

    Spiral Model (1986), Rapid System Development (1987), and Rapid Application Development (1991) gained traction by focusing on agility to be customer-centric, address demand, build a minimum viable product (MVP) and reduce time to market. It promoted interactions, working software, and customer collaboration over following a plan or processes.

    The spiral model combines the iterative model's small repeated cycles with the waterfall model's linear sequential flow to prioritize risk analysis. You can use the spiral model to ensure software's gradual release and improvement by building prototypes at each phase.


    The spiral model is suitable for large and complex projects that require frequent changes. However, it can be expensive for smaller projects with a limited scope.

    In the 1980s and 1990s, iterative development models started gaining traction as a response to the limitations of the Waterfall model. 

    
    With iterative processes, development phases are interleaved with each other for improved flexibility, efficiency, and more realistic scheduling. Instead of completing the project all at once, one might go through most of the steps with one component at a time. Iterative development also lets developers prioritize the most important features, enabling lower priority ones to be dropped later on if necessary.

    Early proponents of iterative methods included Spiral Development (introduced by Barry Boehm in 1986), which combined elements of prototyping and risk management.

6. Agile (2000s - Present)

    Agile software development refers to a group of software methodologies, all based on iterative development.

    The Agile Manifesto (2001) formalized a new approach to software development, emphasizing flexibility, collaboration, and frequent iterations and product delivery over rigid, linear processes.
    
    Agile is now the most popular software development methodology. It is a highly dynamic and iterative approach where you do not need the complete set of requirements to start with. You can develop some features and check customer response before taking the next step. 


    Agile methods like Scrum and Extreme Programming (XP) became widely popular for their ability to accommodate changing requirements and deliver software incrementally.

    Scrum (1993) includes sprint planning, daily meet-ups, reviews and demos, and a retrospective at the end of each sprint. The cycle lasts 2–4 weeks. 
    
    While Scrum is oriented towards the organization of the process, Extreme Programming XP (1999) targets the technical side. It includes tactics such as continuous integration, pair programming, and test-driven development. 
    
    Agile methods improve software quality and responsiveness to customer needs.

7. DevOps and Continuous Integration (2010s-Present)

    With the rise of web-based applications and cloud computing, DevOps emerged in the mid-2000s as an approach that integrates development and operations. 
    
    DevOps (2009) is an Agile methodology encompassing Development (Dev) and Operations (Ops). It enables end-to-end lifecycle delivery of features, fixes, and updates at frequent intervals. Agile adoption inherently left the Operations department behind with deployments piling up faster than they could be released. This trend ultimately pushed the rise of DevOps

    DevOps emphasizes continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD) processes, allowing for frequent and incremental software releases.
    In this era, SDLCs have become more flexible, with many modern approaches allowing for continuous feedback, frequent updates, and rapid iteration.

    Continuous Integration and Continuous Delivery (CI/CD) falls under DevOps. It automates much of the traditional human intervention needed to get new code tested and pushed out for delivery and deployment. One of the key goals is to place code right into a production environment without stopping any functions from running. 









### Timeline of Key Features

This timeline highlights the progression of software development methodologies, each bringing new practices to improve the efficiency, quality, and collaboration in software projects. From early ad-hoc development and the Waterfall model to modern practices in Agile, DevOps, and Design Thinking, the evolution of methodologies reflects the growing complexity of software systems and the need for better, more adaptive ways to build them. Each methodology formalized specific practices that, together, have shaped the industry and influenced how teams approach software development today.


1. Early Days (Pre-1950s)

    Ad-hoc development (Pre-1950s): Early software development was done without formal methodologies. Developers wrote machine code directly or used assembly languages. There were no established practices, and code was often written in a one-off manner for specific tasks. Though no practices are outlined,

2. Structured Programming (Late 1950s-1970s)


        Control Structures: Emphasis on using structured control flow (e.g., loops, conditionals) to improve readability and maintainability.
        
        Modular Programming: Breaking down code into smaller, reusable modules or functions to enhance clarity and reuse.
        
        Top-Down Design: Starting with the high-level design of a system and progressively breaking it down into smaller components.
        
        Code Readability: Encouraged writing clear and understandable code, avoiding "spaghetti code."

3. Waterfall Model (1970s)

        Sequential Phases: The project moves through distinct, sequential phases: Requirements Analysis → System Design → Implementation → Testing → Deployment → Maintenance.

        Clear Documentation: Emphasis on comprehensive documentation at each phase to ensure clarity and traceability.
        
        Strict Phase Completion: Each phase must be completed before the next phase begins, with little iteration.

4. Prototyping Model (1970s-1980s)

        Rapid Prototyping: Create an early version of the system (prototype) to visualize and gather feedback from clients early in development.

        Iterative Refinement: Use client feedback to improve and refine the prototype.
        
        Client Involvement: Encourage continuous involvement of the client to clarify requirements and adjust the prototype.

5. Spiral Model (1986)

        Risk Management: Emphasis on identifying and addressing risks early in the development cycle.
        
        Iterative Development: A series of iterative cycles (spirals), with each cycle involving planning, risk analysis, engineering, testing, and evaluation.
        
        Prototyping & Feedback: Build prototypes and gather feedback at each iteration to guide further development.

6. Rapid Application Development (RAD) (1980s)

        Prototyping: Emphasis on building prototypes quickly to meet client needs and gather early feedback.
        
        Time-boxed Iterations: Short development cycles (often 1-3 months) to deliver working software frequently.
        
        Client Involvement: Continuous collaboration with clients to gather feedback during each iteration.
        
        Component-based Construction: Reuse of existing software components to speed up development.

7. V-Model (1980s - 90s)

        Validation and Verification: Each phase of development has a corresponding testing phase (e.g., unit testing, integration testing, system testing) that ensures the correctness of the product.

        Parallel Phases: Development and testing activities are closely aligned and performed in parallel, forming a "V" shape.

        Test-First Approach: Testing is planned and implemented from the beginning, not as an afterthought.

8. Agile Methodologies (1990s-Present)


        Iterative and Incremental Development: Software is developed in short cycles (iterations), with each cycle delivering a potentially shippable product.

        User Stories: Requirements are captured as user stories (short descriptions of features from the user’s perspective) rather than detailed specifications.

        Continuous Client Feedback: Close collaboration with the client and regular feedback loops to adjust and improve the product.

        Adaptive Planning: Emphasis on flexibility and responsiveness to changing requirements.

        Cross-Functional Teams: Teams are composed of members with all necessary skills (developers, testers, designers) to handle a feature or iteration.

9. Scrum (1995-Present)

        Sprint Cycles: Development is organized into short, fixed-length iterations called sprints (usually 2-4 weeks).
        Daily Standups: Daily meetings where team members discuss progress, challenges, and next steps.

        Product Backlog: A prioritized list of features or tasks, maintained and updated throughout the project.

        Scrum Roles: Defined roles such as Scrum Master (facilitates the process), Product Owner (represents the customer), and Development Team (builds the product).

        Sprint Review & Retrospective: At the end of each sprint, a review meeting with stakeholders is held to showcase the work done, followed by a retrospective meeting to reflect on process improvements.

10. Extreme Programming (XP) (1999-Present)


        Pair Programming: Two developers work together at the same computer, with one writing code and the other reviewing it in real-time.
        
        Test-Driven Development (TDD): Write automated tests before writing the code that satisfies those tests.
        
        Continuous Integration: Code is integrated and tested frequently (usually multiple times a day) to detect problems early.
        
        Collective Code Ownership: The whole team is responsible for all parts of the code, and anyone can modify any part of the codebase at any time.
        
        Simple Design: Focus on writing the simplest code that works, avoiding unnecessary complexity.
        
        Frequent Releases: Deliver working software at frequent intervals (usually every few weeks) to gather feedback and make adjustments.
        
        Customer Involvement: The customer is continuously involved in the development process, providing feedback and clarification on requirements.

11. Feature-Driven Development (FDD) (1997-Present)


        Feature Lists: The project is broken down into small, manageable features, each of which is implemented and delivered as part of the overall system.
        Incremental Development: Features are developed and delivered in short, iterative cycles.
        
        Modeling: A clear model of the system is developed and maintained to guide feature development.
        
        Design and Build by Feature: Features are developed based on their design and implementation needs, ensuring alignment with business goals.

12. Lean Software Development (2003-Present)


        Eliminate Waste: Focus on removing activities that do not add value to the customer, such as excessive documentation and delays.
        Continuous Improvement: Encourage regular reflection and process improvements to increase efficiency.
        Empowerment: Teams are empowered to make decisions and solve problems without micromanagement.
        Just-In-Time Development: Focus on delivering features when they are needed, rather than planning everything upfront.
        Minimize Batch Size: Break tasks into smaller batches to deliver software more quickly and reduce delays.

13. DevOps (2009-Present)


        Continuous Integration (CI): Frequently integrate code into a shared repository to detect issues early.

        Continuous Delivery (CD): Automate the deployment process to enable frequent and reliable delivery of software to production.
        
        Collaboration Between Development and Operations: Promote close collaboration between development and IT operations teams to streamline workflows.
        
        Infrastructure as Code (IaC): Use code to manage and automate infrastructure, making it easier to deploy and scale applications.
        
        Monitoring and Feedback: Continuous monitoring of applications and systems to ensure reliability and gather real-time feedback for improvements.

14. Design Thinking (2010s-Present)


        Empathy with Users: A deep focus on understanding the needs and experiences of end-users.
        
        Prototyping and Iteration: Emphasis on creating low-fidelity prototypes and iterating on them based on user feedback.
        
        Collaboration: Cross-disciplinary collaboration to address complex problems and come up with innovative solutions.
        
        Human-Centered Design: A focus on designing products that are usable, useful, and desirable for users.


















Phases

The system development life cycle framework provides a sequence of activities for system designers and developers to follow. It consists of a set of steps or phases in which each phase of the SDLC uses the results of the previous one.





1. Preliminary analysis
Begin with a preliminary analysis, propose alternative solutions, describe costs and benefits, and submit a preliminary plan with recommendations.

    Conduct the preliminary analysis:
    Discover the organization's objectives and the nature and scope of the problem under study. Even if a problem refers only to a small segment of the organization itself, find out what the objectives of the organization itself are. Then see how the problem being studied fits in with them.

    Propose alternative solutions:
    After digging into the organization's objectives and specific problems, several solutions may have been discovered. However, alternate proposals may still come from interviewing employees, clients, suppliers, and/or consultants. Insight may also be gained by researching what competitors are doing.

    Cost benefit analysis:
    Analyze and describe the costs and benefits of implementing the proposed changes. In the end, the ultimate decision on whether to leave the system as is, improve it, or develop a new system will be guided by this and the rest of the preliminary analysis data.

    Systems analysis, requirements definition:
    Define project goals into defined functions and operations of the intended application. This involves the process of gathering and interpreting facts, diagnosing problems, and recommending improvements to the system. Project goals will be further aided by analysis of end-user information needs and the removal of any inconsistencies and incompleteness in these requirements.


   This stage involves conducting interviews with stakeholders to identify the desired features, functionalities, and overall scope of the software. 
    
    
    The development team works closely with the client to analyze existing systems and workflows, determine technical feasibility, and define project milestones.

    Requirements are synthesized to create a Software Requirement Specification (SRS) document.
    
    The SRS document defines the purpose, product, and technical requirements of a project.
    The document sets expectations and defines common goals that aid in project planning.

    Equipped with the SRS, an organization then estimates costs, allocates resources, creates a schedule to achieve their goals.
    
A series of steps followed by the developer include:
  Collection of facts: Obtain end user requirements through documentation, client interviews, observation, and questionnaires.

  Scrutiny of the existing system: Identify pros and cons of the current system in-place, so as to carry forward the pros and avoid the cons in the new system.

  Analysis of the proposed system: Find solutions to the shortcomings described in step two and prepare the specifications using any specific user proposals.

2. Systems design:
At this step, desired features and operations are described in detail, including screen layouts, business rules, process diagrams, pseudocode, and other documentation.

    In the design phase, software engineers analyze requirements outlined in the SRS and identify the best solutions to create the software. 

    For example, they may 
    evaluate integrating pre-existing modules 
    identify technology choices
    assess various development tools 
    determine how to integrate the new software into any existing IT infrastructure
    establish a software architecture 
    consider User interface (UI) and user experience (UX) to ensure usability, intuitiveness, and visual appeal.

    This results in a Design Document Specification (DDS). The DDS is reviewed by all stakeholders based on a set of criteria.

3. Development:
code is written.

    Developers analyze the requirements to identify smaller coding tasks they can do daily to achieve the final result.
    
    Engaging in cycles of implementing, reviewing, and quality assurance.

    If it meets requirements outlined by the SRS, then the product or feature is integrated into the software.

4. Integration and testing:
All the modules are brought together into a special testing environment, then checked for errors, bugs, and interoperability.

6. Acceptance, installation, deployment:
This is the final stage of initial development, where the software is put into production and runs actual business.

    When teams develop software, they code and test on a different copy of the software than the one that the users have access to. The software that customers use is called production, while other copies are said to be in the build environment, or testing environment.

    The deployment phase includes several tasks to move the latest build copy to the production environment, such as packaging, environment configuration, and installation.
    
    The development team assists the client in setting up the software environment, migrating data if necessary, and configuring the system. User training and documentation are also provided to ensure a smooth transition and enable users to maximize the software's potential.

7. Maintenance:
During the maintenance stage of the SDLC, the system is assessed/evaluated to ensure it does not become obsolete. This is also where changes are made to initial software.

    Maintenance is an ongoing phase that involves supporting and updating the software as needed, throughout the complete lifespan, as long as it remains useful to the customer and cost-effective for the vendor to maintain.

    In the maintenance phase, among other tasks, the team fixes bugs, resolves customer issues, and manages any other software changes required. In addition, the team monitors overall system performance, security, and user experience to identify new ways to improve the existing software.
    
    This phase also involves providing technical support to end users and addressing their queries or concerns.

8. Evaluation:
This is where the system that was developed, as well as the entire process, is evaluated.

Some companies do not view this as an official stage of the SDLC, while others consider it to be an extension of the maintenance stage, and may be referred to in some circles as post-implementation review. 

Some of the questions that need to be answered include if the newly implemented system meets the initial business requirements and objectives, if the system is reliable and fault-tolerant, and if it functions according to the approved functional requirements. In addition to evaluating the software that was released, it is important to assess the effectiveness of the development process. If there are any aspects of the entire process (or certain stages) that management is not satisfied with, this is the time to improve.

9. Disposal: 
Plans are developed for discontinuing the use of system information, hardware, and software and making the transition to a new system. The purpose here is to properly move, archive, discard, or destroy information, hardware, and software that is being replaced, in a manner that prevents any possibility of unauthorized disclosure of sensitive data. The disposal activities ensure proper migration to a new system. Particular emphasis is given to proper preservation and archiving of data processed by the previous system. All of this should be done in accordance with the organization's security requirements.







Modern approaches work in periodic development cycles called incremental iterations.

We start an iteration with a vision or an idea. We determine the fundamental parts with the greatest utility to the stakeholders that can be completed in a single development cycle, aka an iteration. At the end of the iteration, we have code that works that we can release to our users. We then begin the next iteration, implementing more features that are released as soon as this iteration is complete. Changes to the increment, typically redirection based on stakeholder feedback or evolving market situations, are collected at the end of every iteration and implemented in subsequent iterations.

The basic idea behind the modern approach is to develop a system through repeated cycles (iterative) and in smaller portions at a time (incremental), allowing software developers to take advantage of what was learned during development of earlier parts or versions of the system. Learning comes from both the development and use of the system, where possible key steps in the process start with a simple implementation of a subset of the software requirements and iteratively enhance the evolving versions until the full system is implemented. At each iteration, design modifications are made and new functional capabilities are added



Deployment is the process of making sure the software is installed in the right location, that it is secured to prevent any hacker attacks, and making sure that privileges are given to the software so that it can read and write files as needed. When the code is deployed, we usually want to test it again to make sure that everything is still working the way it is intended. Deploying the code is usually done in several steps. Developers will typically have a server that's used during the development process. This is called the development server, and during development, the code can be executed and tested on this server. It will often come with a development database that, if the application we develop uses a database, is used to simulate the real data the application handles. Still, it can be modified any way the developer wants as it is detached from the real data the users see and work with.

Before the code is moved to the production server, that is, the server the application users will use to run the application, the code is usually moved to an intermediate server, often referred to as a staging server. The role of this server is to be as much like the production server as possible so that the new code can be tested alongside the code that was already released. The idea is to make sure that everything runs smoothly and that no bugs appear when the new code hits the live production server. The staging server also has a database. The data in this database is often copied from the production database to make sure that everything is a mirror of the real server. Finally, when the team is confident that everything works correctly, the code is moved to the production server. Apart from moving the code, other adjustments might be needed, such as adding things to the production database that the new software version will need, adding other applications and code libraries that are used by the new code, and so on.

We must also have a strategy for how we can undo this deployment if needed. No matter how thorough our test has been, when the code hits production and users start to use it, things that we never imagined begin surfacing. Users might behave in unpredicted ways, and there might be differences in the production environment compared to the staging environment that we overlooked. We always hope for the best, but we will need a plan for what we will do if things backfire. What we want is a way to undo all the changes and go back to what we had before the deployment as quickly as possible. This is called a rollback strategy. We can accomplish such a rollback in several ways. One of them would be to take a backup of the production database before the deployment occurs. This way, we can ensure that we have a snapshot of how all the data in the application was, before the release of the new code.

The actual code is usually deployed to the server with the help of a VCS, so rolling back the actual code to a previous version is rather simple. The steps involved in deploying code to the production environment are often automated, meaning that different tools and applications take care of all the steps.

Deployment Automation
It is often a good idea to automate as many of the deployment steps as possible. The reason is that there are usually many steps involved that need to be done in the correct order, a job ideally suited for automation. Depending on the complexity of the system, we can use several tools that will help us accomplish automated deployment. Automating deployment will ensure that we have high-quality code since the automation process typically runs tests and lets the code through to the production server that passes the tests. When the code is deployed, the work with it is not over. Deployed code must be maintained.


Code maintenance
A typical software developer will spend more time maintaining existing code than writing new exciting features. This is not very strange if we think about it.

First, the lifespan of an application is typically much longer than the time it took to develop it. This means that there are more and more software out there that are running and performing their tasks. Programs that are used will always need maintenance as users will find bugs that we must fix, new features will be added to the application as demands from users change, and old features must be updated and improved.

This means that developers spend most of their time in old code, fixing and updating it. This can be code written decades ago by someone who has left the company a long time ago, or it can be written by you just the other week.

If you think ahead to what your life as a programmer will look like, you will most likely imagine yourself writing new, exciting software that uses the latest tools and features that are out there. Still, the fact is that it is much more likely that you will be poking around in code written a long time ago, in a version of the language that was released when you were in preschool.

At times, you will have the chance to write that brand-new, cool, and shiny piece of software, but remember, if you do a good job, that code will live on and will need to be maintained by others (or yourself) in the future. That is the life of a software developer. This means that we need to ensure that the code we write is as maintainable as possible.

A skilled software developer is someone who writes highly maintainable code. This means that the code is clear, easy to understand, and easy to change, without the risk of people introducing some unwanted side effects in the application.

There is a simple rule we can follow to help us create maintainable code: the boy scout rule. The legend of the boy scout rule says: Leave the campground cleaner than you found it.

In the book Clean Code, the author, Robert C. Martin, also known as Uncle Bob, transformed this rule so that it could be applied to code. He states that if we apply this rule when we maintain our code, we should always leave our code a little cleaner and better each time we maintain it so that its quality will increase over time. We can rephrase the boy scout rule in relation to software development so that it says, Always leave the code you're editing a little better than you found it. The changes do not have to be huge. You will not have to rewrite large sections of code. If you change a variable name to something that better describes the value it is holding, or adding documentation to a piece of code where it was missing previously will increase the code's quality slightly.



Another focus in many programming methodologies is the idea of trying to catch issues such as security vulnerabilities and bugs as early as possible (shift-left testing) to reduce the cost of tracking and fixing them.
